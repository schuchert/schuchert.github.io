## For the Impatient (tl;dr)

**The Golden Rule**: Do not try to change or stop the habit. Just observe it.

1. **Target**: Identify a habit you want to change.
2. **Observe**: Start practicing noticing the habit.
3. **Classify**: Categorize the event using only these phrases: "I just did it," "I am doing it," or "I am about to do
   it."
4. **Anticipate**: Over time, watch your observation often migrate backward from after the habit, to during the habit,
   to before the habit.
5. **Fail**: When you forget to observe, especially hours later, congratulations. Realizing you forgot means your
   observation skills are starting to get stronger.

### Recommendations

* Really, only observe, do not worry about change. Get to know the habit.
* Start with one habit.
* Start small. No smaller. Even smaller still. The smaller you start, the quicker you'll notice improvement.
* YOU WILL FAIL, and you will succeed. To have actual results, both are required.

> **A note on scope:** This is one tool among many, and it won't be the right one for everyone or every habit. It's a
> way to discover that you can participate in how you already are, starting with everyday habits like snacking,
> scrolling, nail biting, and interrupting people. Think of it as an opener, not a finish. If a
> habit is causing serious harm (addiction, self-harm, disordered eating, compulsions), treat this as a
> supplement at most, and please involve a doctor, therapist, or support group. Try it, keep what works, and drop what
> doesn't.

## The Physics of "Don't Try to Quit"

The most counterintuitive part of this practice is the only rule that matters: **You must not try to stop the habit.**

If you are trying to quit, you are using willpower. Willpower requires massive energy, creates guilt when you fail, and
most importantly, it triggers resistance.

Think about physical stretching. If you aggressively yank a tight hamstring, your nervous system panics and contracts
the muscle to protect it. You actually create *more* tension. Habits seem to behave similarly. If you try to
aggressively stop one, it can feel like a threat to a familiar pathway, and something in you braces against the attempt,
often reinforcing the habit.

By telling yourself, "I'm not trying to quit, I'm just observing," you may sidestep that bracing. When the urge arises,
you have full permission to execute the habit—eat the snack, doomscroll, bite your nails. Your *only*
requirement is that you run the observation script.

## The Heisenberg Effect: Why Observation is the Intervention

You might be wondering: *If I'm not trying to stop the habit, how does it ever change?*

I'm using "Heisenberg" loosely here, as shorthand for the observer effect. To observe anything, you have to exchange
energy with it (to see something, photons have to bounce off it), so the observer and the observed both change. Neither
is untouched by the act, and each arises in dependence on the other.

Programmers have a name for the software version: a "Heisenbug," a glitch that disappears or changes behavior the moment
you try to look at it, because the act of logging interrupts the system's timing.

Psychology has a plainer name for this: *reactivity of self-monitoring*. Tracking a behavior tends to change it, even
when you aren't trying to change it. Human habits seem to work in a similar way. Habits run on automatic, mindless autopilot. But the moment you pause and
say, "I am doing it," you shift gears. Loosely speaking, you pull attention away from the automatic, unconscious part of
your brain and route it into the conscious, executive part.

You don't have to violently break the habit. Just by shining the flashlight of observation on it, you disrupt its
automatic timing. The measurement *is* the intervention.

## The Timeline of Awareness

As you practice this, your observation will often migrate backward through time. You are tracking the habit upstream to
its source. It happens in three distinct phases:

* **Phase 1: Retroactive ("I just did it.")**
  At first, you won't realize you did the habit until it's over. The bag of chips is empty. You say, *"I just did it."*
  Do not beat yourself up. Forgetting, and then remembering later, is a massive win. You are successfully building the
  observation muscle.
* **Phase 2: Concurrent ("I am doing it.")**
  Eventually, the gap shortens. You will "wake up" right in the middle of the action. You have the chip in your hand.
  You say, *"I am doing it."* You can still eat the chip! Just log the event.
* **Phase 3: Proactive ("I am about to do it.")**
  This is the breakthrough. One day, you will reach for the cabinet, and before your hand touches the handle, the
  observation will fire: *"I am about to do it."*

When you hit Phase 3, you are standing in the exact structural gap between the trigger and the response. You aren't
operating on autopilot anymore. Because you are holding the moment in pure observation, the old wiring seems to starve.
The habit doesn't have to be broken; in my experience it often just stops running.

## When It Plateaus

Observation plateaus. Eventually the labels become a habit too, and you can say "I am doing it" while ignoring
it completely. That's not a failure of the practice. It's the next thing to observe: *I just noticed that I labeled it
and didn't care.* The practice points at itself, so it's turtles all the way down, in a good way.

Phase 3 also gives you a choice point you didn't have before. That is where Weinberg's advice fits: add a new habit
that interrupts the old one. You might take a breath, drink water, or stand up. Observation gets you to the gap, and
you decide what to put in it. If the habit has real momentum or real consequences, this is also a good time to bring in
a coach, a therapist, or a support group.

## The One Trap: Don't Ask "Why?"

There is only one way to crash this system, and it happens when you try to get too smart.

Instead of holding the raw, simple observation ("I am doing it"), you might be tempted to ask *why* you are doing it.
You start generating a story: *"I'm reaching for a snack because I had a hard day, and my boss was a jerk, and I'm
stressed, and I deserve a break."*

Do not do this. Asking "why" is a trap. It triggers your brain's rationalization engine. When you start making up a
psychological narrative about your habit, you aren't observing anymore—you are just feeding the habit with excuses. You
get pulled right back into the autopilot loop.

Keep it sterile. Keep it simple. Stick strictly to the three phrases: *I just did it. I am doing it. I am about to do
it.*

Think of it as a weather report: "Light rain. Wind from the west." Nobody gets scolded or congratulated. That goes for
your tone, inside and out, and for your words. Use as little emotional charge as you can, because charge is a hook for
the autopilot. If three phrases is still too many, simplify further. Labeling a thought as just "thinking" works the
same way, and I've found that simpler is usually better.

## The Lineage: Software, Systems, and Śūnyatā

If you are wondering why this specific protocol works, it helps to look at where it comes from. I tend to mix domains
because the patterns of complex systems rhyme across whatever substrate they run on. Treat each of these as a model, not
a proof.

This protocol grew out of several sources, with Śūnyatā as a bottom-up reasoning tool and systems thinking underneath.

1. **Software (Aspects and Heisenbugs)** In software, Aspect-Oriented Programming (AOP) lets you "weave" an observer
   (logging, say) around existing code without rewriting it. In my own work with AspectJ, weaving in logging changed
   thread timings and behavior, and I've seen logs fill a hard drive and take a system down. Observation is never free,
   and it is never neutral. The habit is running on automatic (loosely, in the basal ganglia). Injecting a verbal log,
   "I am about to do this," is the same kind of intervention, and it changes the system's timing.
2. **Human Systems (Weinberg and Satir)** I spent years studying human systems under Jerry Weinberg and Virginia Satir.
   Satir’s change model dictates that a system remains locked in the status quo until a "Foreign Element" introduces
   chaos, forcing a reorganization. The simple act of neutral observation is that Foreign Element. Weinberg constantly
   reinforced that observing a system inherently changes it, and that interventions fail when you try to force change
   without observing the baseline first. Weinberg also taught that to change a habit, you add a new one that interrupts
   the old one. The observation phrase is that new habit. That is why the Golden Rule is to simply observe, not quit.
3. **Non-Dualism and Śūnyatā** Phase 3 ("I am about to do it") reminds me of Śūnyatā, or emptiness, offered here as a
   somatic, behavioral metaphor and not as a doctrinal claim. Śūnyatā points at dependent arising: nothing, including a
   habit, stands alone. The trigger, the urge, and the response each arise in dependence on conditions, and the gap
   between them is where that becomes visible. Śūnyatā also says that emptiness is itself empty. Every model here,
   including this one, is wrong, and some are useful anyway. You don't have to fight the habit in that gap; you can
   stand there and observe the system state. The same goes for the labels in this practice. They are tools for seeing,
   and they go wrong when we mistake them for the thing they point at. "I am doing it" describes an event. It says
   nothing about who you are.
4. **Labeling Thoughts (David Nichtern)** The three phrases are cousins of a mindfulness instruction I learned from
   David Nichtern: when a thought arises, label it simply "thinking" and let it go. Labeling is a way of seeing the
   thing without joining in. Here the label is aimed at a habit instead of a thought.
5. **Self-Reference (Gödel, Escher, Bach)** I love self-reference and Hofstadter's *GEB*. This practice is a habit of
   noticing habits, so it points at itself. I think of it as an awareness (or metacognition) boot loader: you install
   the habit of observing habits, a small program whose only job is to start a bigger one. The aim is for you to see
   your own power to watch, and so to steer, your own machinery. It's meant to be a bit counter-culture. You don't need
   an app, a guru, or a streak counter.

I derived this from the sources above and from my own practice, not from the research literature on habit change. Treat
it as one person's field notes.

### Prior Art (Found After the Fact)

None of this is original. At most, the synthesis is. Since arriving at it, I've learned that several people reached
neighboring ideas from other directions, which is a good sign for a model:

* **Habit reversal training** (Azrin and Nunn, 1973) starts with *awareness training*: learning to notice a habit as it
  happens, and often just before, before trying to replace it. Here, awareness training is the whole program, and it
  doubles as the boot loader for noticing everything else.
* **Urge surfing** (Alan Marlatt) teaches people to observe a craving rise and fall without acting on it or fighting it.
* **Mindfulness for cravings** (Judson Brewer and colleagues) has people notice and name what a craving feels like,
  including in studies on smoking and overeating.
* **Self-monitoring reactivity** is an established finding in behavioral psychology (see Nelson and Hayes, 1981):
  recording or noticing a behavior often changes it, which is the plain-language version of "the measurement is the
  intervention."
* **Affect labeling** (Lieberman and colleagues, 2007) found that putting a feeling into words reduces the brain's
  emotional reactivity to it, which supports the use of simple, neutral labels.
* **Noting practice** in the Burmese vipassana tradition (Mahasi Sayadaw) labels each experience as it arises, much like
  Nichtern's "thinking."
* **Pointing and calling** (*shisa kanko*) is used on Japanese railways: workers point at a signal and say aloud what
  they see, which interrupts autopilot and has been studied for reducing errors.

Different fields, different vocabularies, the same move: make the automatic visible, and do it without a fight.
